About

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Psychology at Florida International University, where I work in the quantitative psychology area.

What I value most in research is curiosity, asking questions and then asking the next one. Before graduate school I spent five years at Northwell Health working on clinical trials of alcohol treatment, where participants reported on their drinking every day. Those studies raised questions I could not answer with the models I knew, and that is a big part of why I went on to a Ph.D. in quantitative psychology. Much of my current work, from the alcohol projects to the loneliness studies I started during my postdoc at Cornell, still begins with a question that came up in a real study.

I work closely with researchers who study emotion, addiction, and mental health, often from the very start of a project. Together we think about what a study can ask, how to design it, and which model fits the theory. Sometimes this leads to a new way of framing the question altogether.

Outside of research, I like to rally on the tennis court just for fun, go hiking, read novels, and spend time with family and close friends.

Research

Most of my methods work began with a question from a real study, and I bring those methods back to substantive questions about loneliness and drinking.

Methods

New frameworks for studying change in daily life

Data collected many times a day let us ask when, how often, and for how long, not only who. I develop models that make these questions testable, and I work with substantive researchers from the start so the design and the analysis fit the theory.

  • Separating whether something happens from how much it happens (zero inflation)
  • How emotions carry over from one moment to the next
  • Planning studies with enough power for the effects that matter
  • Mediation and intervention effects over time
Empirical

Loneliness and drinking in daily life

Loneliness and drinking both rise and fall within a day. I study how lonely moments change the way people read social threat, when time alone helps rather than hurts, and what drives drinking and change during treatment.

  • Loneliness and social threat detection
  • Solitude and well-being
  • Daily drinking, craving, and treatment
  • Emotion dynamics
